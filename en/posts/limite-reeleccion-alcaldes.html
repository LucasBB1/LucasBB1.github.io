The end of entrenchment: term limits and municipal competition in Chile, 2012–2024

Lucas Bottinelli Baltra (ORCID 0009-0000-4865-7261) · MA candidate in Political Science, Pontificia Universidad Católica de Chile
Working Paper No. 2 · November 2026 · Not peer reviewed · Full paper in Spanish

Full paper (PDF, Spanish) Data and code DOI: 10.5281/zenodo.23877074

In 2020, Law No. 21,238 limited mayors to three consecutive terms and counted the terms already served. It therefore applied immediately: 96 mayors could not run again in 2021 and another 69 in 2024. Some had governed their municipality since 1992.

This working paper asks what changes in a municipal election when the mayor leaves because of the legal limit rather than by choice. The distinction matters because voluntary exits are not random, while the limit works through a rule the mayor does not control. This entry summarises the findings; the full paper develops the theoretical framework, the method and the limitations.

Data and methods

The analysis uses official Electoral Service (SERVEL) results for the 2012, 2016, 2021 and 2024 mayoral elections in all 345 municipalities, together with the 24 mayoral replacements during those terms, documented case by case. Terms served are counted according to SERVEL's criterion, which considers a term served when more than half of it was completed, as elected mayor or as replacement. The incumbency advantage is estimated with a regression discontinuity in close elections, and the effect of the limit with stacked difference-in-differences by wave of application (2021 and 2024). All processing is reproducible in R.

Five findings

1. The incumbency advantage is large

Winning the mayoralty by a narrow margin increases the probability of winning the next election by 33 percentage points. Near the threshold, narrow losers win the next election in one case out of four, and narrow winners in one out of two. The result holds across bandwidths and does not appear at placebo thresholds. That advantage is what the limit removes from competition.

2. The limit opened up competition

Municipalities with a term-limited mayor added about 1.5 mayoral candidates relative to the previous election, roughly 45% more. The effect is almost identical in 2021, with voluntary voting (1.43), and in 2024, with compulsory voting (1.75), which makes it less likely to reflect a particular moment.

3. The entrenched mayor deterred challengers

While their mayor was running, the municipalities that would later be term-limited had fewer candidates than other municipalities with an incumbent running. This is what the deterrence theory predicts: challengers avoid a strong incumbent and wait for an open seat. Once the mayor was kept off the ballot by law, that pent-up competition emerged all at once. Two elections before the limit, both groups evolved in parallel.

4. A forced exit attracts more candidates than a voluntary one

Every election without an incumbent attracts more candidates, but not to the same extent. A seat opened by the limit adds 0.58 more candidates than one the mayor leaves by choice. One possible explanation is that a mayor who retires voluntarily chooses the moment and can arrange the succession.

5. The limit does not punish the outgoing camp

Without an incumbent, the outgoing mayor's political family keeps the mayoralty in about one municipality out of five, whether the exit is by law or voluntary, compared with three out of five when the incumbent runs. The difference between the two types of exit is not statistically significant. The limit increases turnover because it multiplies open seats, not because it hurts the outgoing mayor's camp: in Chilean municipal politics, continuity rests more on the person than on the party.

Scope

The 2021 term-limited mayors were identified from a public list, because SERVEL does not publish the 2008 results in data format, and mayoral replacements were reconstructed from the press. The study measures how many candidates run, not their quality or the later performance of municipal administrations. Ahead of 2028, 46 mayors will not be able to run again, a third wave smaller than the previous ones.
