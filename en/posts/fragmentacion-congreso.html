Beyond the rule: electoral and parliamentary fragmentation in Chile after the end of the binomial system, 2009–2026

Lucas Bottinelli Baltra (ORCID 0009-0000-4865-7261) · MA candidate in Political Science, Pontificia Universidad Católica de Chile
Working Paper No. 4 · November 2026 · Not peer reviewed · Full paper in Spanish

Full paper (PDF, Spanish) Data and code DOI: 10.5281/zenodo.23924456

Between 2009 and 2025, the effective number of parties in the Chamber of Deputies rose from 5.6 to about 10, peaking at 11.6 in 2021. In the same period, the 2015 reform (Law No. 20,840) replaced the binomial system with a proportional system using the D'Hondt method, so a common explanation attributes the rise in fragmentation to the reform.

This working paper asks how much of that rise is due to the new rule for converting votes into seats and how much to the fragmentation of the vote, and whether fragmentation continues after the election, during the legislative term. This entry summarises the findings; the full paper develops the theoretical framework, the method and the limitations.

Data and methods

The analysis uses candidate-level results for deputies and senators from 2009 to 2025 from the Electoral Service (SERVEL), the Chamber's membership register and a reconstruction of senators' party membership from the Library of Congress biographies. The design exploits the fact that the 60 binomial districts are fully nested in the 28 new districts, which makes it possible to apply each rule to the votes of the other period while holding votes constant. The allocation reproduces, candidate by candidate, the 670 deputies elected in the period. All processing is reproducible in R.

Five findings

1. The vote became twice as fragmented

The effective number of parties in votes for the Chamber rose from 7.3 in 2009 to 15.4 in 2021 and 14.2 in 2025. The sharpest change occurred between coalitions. Under the binomial system, the effective number of lists stayed close to 2.6, and in 2021 it reached 6.1.

2. The rule alone does not explain the increase

With the 2009 and 2013 votes, the D'Hondt rule would have produced almost the same Chamber as the binomial system, with differences of +0.2 and −0.1 in the effective number of parliamentary parties. With the 2021 votes, the binomial system would have reduced that number from 11.6 to 8.5, but it would still have brought 17 parties into the Chamber, compared with 8 in 2009 and 2013.

3. The mechanical effect comes from district magnitude

Since the binomial system is the D'Hondt method applied to two-seat districts, the reform did not change the formula but the district magnitude, the allocation of seats among the parties of each list and the size of the Chamber. When these components are separated, almost all the difference between the two rules is due to the move to districts of 3 to 8 seats. The increase to 155 seats and the allocation among parties matter little.

4. Fragmentation continues during the term

The share of deputies who switched party during their term rose from 3% in 2010–2014 to 14% in 2018–2022 and 26% in 2022–2026. In the Senate, it stayed between 5% and 8% until 2022 and rose to 20% in 2022–2026. Many of those changes were towards parties created during the same period.

5. Each term ends more fragmented

In both chambers and in all four periods, the effective number of parties was higher at the end than at the beginning. Fragmentation in Congress thus results from three different processes, the dispersion of the vote, the conversion of votes into seats and party switching during the term.

Scope

The counterfactual holds votes constant, so it measures the mechanical effect of the rule and not the strategic responses of parties and voters. The increase in lists and candidates per district suggests that the reform worked mainly through the supply side. Compulsory voting changed the electorate in 2025, and party membership does not always match the caucus each legislator sits in. Data and code are public, so the analysis can be updated after each election.
