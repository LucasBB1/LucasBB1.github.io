Winners and losers: the roots of democratic dissatisfaction in Chile, 2006–2026

Lucas Bottinelli Baltra, Matías Llorens Rubio and Benjamín Zúñiga Silva · Master's in Political Science, Pontificia Universidad Católica de Chile
Group coursework for Measurement and Dimensional Analysis of Political Data (ICP5006), Professor Soledad Araya · Assignment 2, preliminary results · November 2026

Code and results (GitHub)

Between 2006 and 2026, the share of Chileans satisfied with how democracy works did not fall in a straight line. It ranged between 49% and 63% until 2014, dropped to 32% in 2017, before the 2019 social unrest, and rose to 55% in 2026. This project asks which groups are more and less satisfied and whether those gaps changed over time. It tests four conditions proposed in the literature: having voted for the winner or a loser of the presidential election, socioeconomic position, age, and access to citizenship rights across the territory.

This entry summarises the results of the second course assignment. The full report will be published here after the final submission.

Data and methods

The analysis uses the ten AmericasBarometer (LAPOP) waves for Chile between 2006 and 2026. The dependent variable is item PN4, which asks about satisfaction with the way democracy works in Chile, recoded as satisfied or very satisfied versus the rest. The models are logistic regressions with wave fixed effects and each survey's sampling design, estimated on the nine face-to-face waves. The 2021 telephone wave is only used in the national series. The whole analysis is reproducible in R from the repository.

Four results

1. Having voted for the winner is the strongest condition

Those who voted for the sitting president have odds of being satisfied 1.9 times higher than those who voted for another candidate. In probabilities, the difference is 15.7 percentage points, almost twice that of any other condition. The gap is positive in every wave with vote data and does not grow over time.

2. Socioeconomic position starts to matter after 2019

Until 2019, education did not differentiate satisfaction and income did so only slightly. In 2023–2026, people with secondary or tertiary education and those with higher income become clearly more satisfied. A Wald test confirms that both effects change across periods.

3. The advantage of older people reverses

Until 2019, people aged 60 or older were the most satisfied. In 2023–2026, the youngest are the most satisfied. Such a rapid change is hard to explain by each generation's early socialisation and fits better with the idea that evaluations of the present weigh more.

4. Territory works the opposite way to what was expected

Lower satisfaction was expected where access to state services is weaker. However, until 2014 the rural population was more satisfied than the urban one, and residents of the Metropolitan Region less satisfied than those in the rest of the country. Since 2017 those gaps have narrowed. The territorial measures capture only part of the concept, so the result does not support the hypothesis, but it does not rule it out either.

What changed

The central result is not a general rise in inequality in satisfaction but a change in who is satisfied. Until the middle of the last decade, older people, rural residents and those outside Santiago were more satisfied. In 2023–2026, people with more education and income, the young and men are.

Scope

The vote is measured with recalled vote, which over-represents winners, and it was not asked in 2026, so part of the recent change may reflect the composition of the new winning electorate. Rural samples are small, and the results are associations, not causal effects. These results are preliminary and may change in the final submission.
